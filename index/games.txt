Realmfold — A tile-drafting game for one to four built on a single trade you can feel on the very first turn: the best tile on the table costs you next round's first pick. A short row of doublets is dealt face up, sorted by number. On your turn you take one and immediately lay it on your own five-by-five demesne, matching land to land, or against the Motte in the middle which matches anything — one half touching is enough. Then the row is dealt again, and the order everybody picks in is simply this round's claims sorted lowest first. So the fat three-coronet Coomb at the bottom of the row is never free. Taking it hands the whole of next round's initiative to whoever settled for the junk, and taking the junk buys you the first two picks of a row you have not seen yet. That is the entire game, and the screen prices it for you: under every doublet, live and recomputed as your rivals claim, is the slot that claiming it would buy. Scoring is what stops this being a race for the prettiest board. A connected region of one land scores its size multiplied by its coronets, so eight cells of Wold with nothing on them are worth precisely nothing, and three cells of Coomb carrying three coronets are worth nine. The consequence is that the same doublet is worth eight points to the player with three Orchard cells already exposed and two to everybody else — which is where taking a tile away from somebody starts to matter more than taking it for yourself. Twelve doublets fill exactly twenty-four cells, so a perfect fill is real and pays ten, and from about round eight it is no longer greed stopping you but geometry. The board tells you that too: because every doublet covers one dark square and one light one, any empty pocket with unequal parity can never be filled by anything, and those squares are hatched out live as you play, which turns the last four rounds from guesswork into a puzzle you can see. Three modes that change what the game is rather than how hard it is. Croft is the tight one described above. Shire moves to seven by seven and changes what a legal move even is — a doublet with the same land on both halves may be founded anywhere at all, touching nothing — so you scatter colonies across open ground and grow them into each other before the room runs out, and six cells in one region pays three. Holt keeps Croft's board and Croft's scoring and inverts the incentive: whoever claims LAST opens the round by burning one doublet out of the match entirely, before anybody picks, so going last is a veto rather than a punishment. That third mode is also the clearest example of the simulation overruling the design. It was built on the theory that the veto would be worth paying tempo for, and a sweep of the weighting showed it is not — a bot that chases the veto plays measurably WORSE than one that ignores it — so the weighting ships at zero and the mode earns its place on what the burn actually does to a match rather than on the story that was told about it. The same simulation caught something worse in Shire, which was specified with sixteen doublets: at that density there is so much room that everything is placeable everywhere, the draft collapses to grabbing coronets, and a bot blind to pick order lost nothing at all — the game's central mechanic was simply inert in that mode, and the player holding the first slot finished three and a half points ahead. Eighteen doublets fixed both, and the obvious fix, removing the colony rule, was tried first and barely moved it. Alone you play a real opponent rather than a placeholder, at three strengths that are a genuine ladder: the strongest takes eighty-eight per cent of games off the weakest and still beats the middle one clearly. Playing friends needs no server and no account, and because nothing in this game is hidden — every demesne, every row and every claim is public — nobody is in charge either. Each browser simply replays the same list of moves and arrives at the same table, so whoever created the room leaving is a repaint rather than a crisis. Every message carries the whole match so far rather than the latest move, which makes a dropped packet not merely survivable but unobservable. If somebody's phone dies the room does not stall: once a turn has been open long enough and that seat has gone quiet, every remaining browser independently plays the same move for it — the opponent is budgeted in search steps rather than milliseconds precisely so two phones of different speeds reach the same answer — and the match runs to a proper finish with a full result for everyone, including the player who left. The summary shows every player, never just you: each demesne, every region written out as its size times its coronets, the bonuses each of you earned and the ones you missed, and what each of you had to throw away. Torc — A two-player abstract built on a cost you cannot avoid: the only way to score is to give away the piece you were scoring with. You each set your torcs — open metal rings — down on a field of points, and every turn you ride one of them in a straight line. It leaves a stud of your colour behind on the point it came from, and that is the whole engine of the game: you are always spending ground to move. A torc glides over empty points, or jumps one unbroken run of studs and lands on the first empty point past it, and every stud it jumped over turns to its other face. Every one, including your own — which is the rule that turns it from a race into an argument, because the fastest way to reach the far side of the board is often straight through the line you spent four turns building, and the jump that hands you three of their studs also hands them the two of yours it crossed on the way. Get four of your studs in a line, or five on the largest field, and that line comes off the board and scores. It also costs you one of your torcs, and you choose which. Three lines wins. So every point you take makes you slower, thinner and easier to trap, and the endgame is routinely played by two players who have each scored twice and can barely move. The other half of that rule is that a line can appear on your opponent's turn: their jump can turn your studs the right way up, and if it does they have handed you a line you must take and pay a torc for, whether you wanted it or not — which means a well-timed jump is a way of forcing somebody to spend a piece they were saving. Three fields that change the game rather than a difficulty setting. Coil is thirty-one points and three torcs each, a knife-fight where giving up one piece in three genuinely hurts and the board is tight enough to jam to a draw about one game in twelve. Twist is fifty-five points, five torcs and three lines to win — the full shape of it. Hoard is eighty-five points and needs a line of FIVE, which cannot be improvised and has to be set up, over roughly twice the game. That the middle field asks for four rather than five is the single thing the simulation changed, and it changed it against the obvious answer: with a line of five that board clogs, because studs accumulate faster than claims clear them, and between two strong engines somewhere between eighteen and thirty-two per cent of games ended with nobody able to move and thirteen per cent were outright draws. The failure is invisible to a cheap opponent — nought per cent at the budget a fast test can afford, thirty-two at a deeper one — because it is caused by both sides playing WELL and blocking each other's lines, which is precisely the wrong direction for a flaw to run. Two confident fixes were tried first and both failed: fewer torcs barely moved it, and a lower target cured the jam by replacing it with a thirty-nine per cent rate of games the loser never scored in at all. Alone you play a real engine rather than a placeholder: negamax with alpha-beta, iterative deepening and a time budget, scoring positions on lines already half-built, marker majority, how much room each torc has left and — the term that matters most — how many torcs each side still has, so it understands exactly what a point costs. Three tiers that are a genuine ladder rather than the same search with noise on top. Playing a friend needs no server and no account: the two browsers talk straight to each other, and because nothing is hidden and nothing is random neither of them is in charge — both simply step the same rules from the same list of moves. Every message carries the entire game so far rather than the latest move, so a lost packet is not merely survivable but unobservable, and a phone that drops off the network catches up on its own within two seconds. Sides swap every round, a match tally runs across the session, and Play again keeps the room rather than dumping you back to a lobby. If your opponent closes their tab you get a countdown you can see and then a real result, not a frozen board — and that holds whether or not the connection notices they have gone, because twenty seconds of silence counts as evidence on its own. Join by scanning a QR, opening a link, or reading the four-character code aloud. The summary shows both players, never just you: lines taken, jumps made, studs turned over, the longest line each of you took — and the best line each of you never finished, which on a board needing four is usually the whole story of the last few turns. Hearsay — A micro-round of deduction for one to six people, and the whole thing turns on a rule you can learn in a sentence. You are holding one piece of testimony. So is everybody else. Nobody sees anybody's. On your turn you draw one off the docket and play one of the two, and whatever you play happens to somebody in front of the entire room, face up, where it stays. Be the last one standing, or hold the strongest testimony when the docket runs dry. What makes it arithmetic rather than a guess is that the deck is public and fixed and never changes: five Bailiffs, two Clerks, two Witnesses, two Recusals, two Retrials, one Advocate, one Judge, one Verdict. Everything anybody plays lands face up, so subtracting what is on the table from what you know is in the box tells you what the room is still holding — and a panel down the side does that subtraction for you, counting what is left of every rank as it goes. It is the same panel the computer opponent plays from, on purpose, because a deduction game whose opponent knows something the interface will not show you is a game that cheats. The one place this departs from the old parlour game it descends from is the commonest card in the deck. A Bailiff names a rival and a rank; get it right and they are out of the hearing. In the original, getting it wrong is a wasted turn, which makes a third of the deck a lottery ticket. Here a miss still pays: you, and only you, are told whether their testimony is higher or lower than the rank you named. A hand full of Bailiffs stops being a lottery and becomes a binary search, and two calls will usually pin somebody exactly. The hint being private is the point rather than a detail — announced to the table it would be a public good, where you spend the whole turn and everybody else gets the read for free. Whatever you have worked out is printed on that rival's row, so the turn you spent buying it is a turn you can see the value of. That it works is measured rather than claimed: a bot that counts the docket beats an identical bot with the counting switched off by roughly seventy per cent to thirty, heads-up, in every mode. The rest of the deck is the pleasant machinery around it. A Clerk reads somebody's card in secret. A Witness compares privately and dismisses the weaker testimony. A Recusal steps you off the record so nothing can touch you until your next turn. A Retrial makes somebody throw their card away and draw again — and if what they shed is the Verdict, they are out. An Advocate trades hands sight unseen. The Judge does nothing at all, except that you must discard her if you are also holding the Advocate or a Retrial, which means somebody discarding the Judge has just told you something specific about the rest of their hand. And the Verdict, the highest card in the game, has no power whatsoever and loses you the hearing the instant it leaves your hand. Three modes that change what a card is FOR rather than turning a difficulty dial. Inquiry is the reference game, where a card is a weapon and its rank matters mainly because it is what somebody has to name to put you out. On the Record pays nothing for winning at all — you score the rank you are still holding when it closes — so the Advocate and the Retrial stop being ways to hurt people and become ways to trade up, and surviving on a Bailiff is worth one point against somebody else's eight. Full Brief hands you two cards and draws you to three, so every turn is a three-way choice, the Judge's forced discard bites far harder, and you can finally carry a shield alongside the Verdict. At two players three cards are turned face up at the start, because the pool is then small enough that counting it is genuinely possible inside a ninety-second hearing. Being knocked out does not mean watching: you back a rival from the gallery, and if they take the hearing you score for having read it right — worth strictly less than winning, which the balance simulator checks, so going out early stays the worst thing that can happen to you. Solo drops you straight in against two opponents with no lobby and no waiting. Multiplayer is two to six over a shared link with no server and no account: create a room and a friend can scan the QR, open the link, or read the four-character code down the phone. Because hands are genuinely hidden, one browser deals and adjudicates and sends every other browser only what the rules say it may see. If that browser closes mid-hearing the game does not end — whoever takes over rebuilds it from the public record plus everyone's own cards, and the pile still to be drawn is recovered by arithmetic rather than memory, because what is left to draw is exactly what nobody has accounted for. Only the order of it is lost, and the order was never known to anybody, so it is shuffled again and the hearing carries on with the same cards it had. Every player reaches the summary, and the summary shows everybody: what each of you was holding, what each of you played, who backed whom — and the one card set aside at the start that nobody at the table could ever have counted. Lustre — An engine-builder for one to four built on a single idea you can hold in your head from the first turn: a rough spar sitting on your bench is either your engine or your score, and it is never both. While it is rough it does two jobs — it discounts every future spar of the other three colours, and it drives your wheel so you gather more grit each time you reach for the pool. Put it to the wheel and cut it, and it pays its number in lustre and is finished forever: no more discount, no more yield, just a scored card that will never do anything again. Your bench only ever holds six, so something always has to go, and deciding which and when is the entire game. What makes that a decision rather than an arithmetic problem is what conversion costs. There are no turns here. Everyone shares one wheel, and whoever is furthest back on it acts next, so a cheap move buys you another go sooner and an expensive one hands the table two moves while you catch up. Cutting costs ticks on that wheel, and the number of ticks is read off a dial in plain sight — a dial that every point anybody at the table banks pushes further along. Convert early, while nobody has scored, and a spar costs you two ticks. Sit on a beautiful bench waiting for the perfect moment and your rivals will have quietly raised the price on you while you waited, because their scoring is what moves it. The number printed in the circle on a card is both what it will pay you and how far it will shove that dial, so the whole calculation is one figure in one place, and the button along the bottom always spells out the consequence in full before you commit to it. The cost of a spar is only ever the other colours, which is what stops a single-colour rush: three garnet spars discount topaz, malachite and azurite and do nothing whatsoever for the next garnet, and pips cap at three a colour, so the deep column you built to satisfy a patron is also the column that stops helping you. Patrons pay for pips — but cutting is what destroys pips, so an order has to be taken before you start converting, and that ordering constraint is the one piece of forward planning the game asks for. Three modes that change the game rather than a difficulty setting. Open Wheel is the reference version over forty-eight ticks. Dry Lode runs the seam cold: the first grit of every payment is destroyed instead of returning to the pool, so the table slowly impoverishes itself, and a fifth verb lets you pan the spoil heap at a flat rate that your engine does nothing to improve — the one place in the game where being big and being small cost exactly the same. Commission removes cutting altogether; ten named orders are the only thing that scores, filling one costs ticks and burns two of your rough spars, and because everybody is racing the same ten targets it is the sharpest of the three. Solo drops you straight in against two bots with no lobby and no waiting, and they are a real opponent rather than a placeholder: they price every possible conversion against a forecast of how fast the rest of the table will bank, which is the same judgement you are making. Multiplayer is two to four over a shared link, and it has no host at all — nothing in this game is hidden, both decks come off one shared number and every card on the table is face up, so each browser simply replays the same list of moves and arrives at the same table. Every message carries the whole game so far rather than the latest move, which means a dropped packet is not merely survivable but unobservable, somebody joining late is in sync immediately, and somebody closing their laptop cannot pause the round: the players still there fill in for the empty seat and the game finishes properly, with a full breakdown of what everybody did. Seats rotate every round so the small advantage of acting first lands on a different person each time. Join by scanning a QR, opening a link or typing the four-character code. Conduit — A network-drawing race for one to six where everybody is dealt exactly the same four fittings and the only thing that differs is what you did with them. A seven-by-seven plate sits in front of you with twelve vents printed around its rim, each one either a culvert or a filament, and the two never meet except through a single scarce fitting. Every round deals four pieces of pipe — straights, elbows, tees, a coupler, a crossing — identical for every player at the table, and you draw all four onto your own plate. What makes it a connection game rather than a jigsaw is that every side of every square is a typed slot. A culvert end can only meet a culvert end, a filament end only a filament end, and a blank wall only another blank wall — so you cannot park a solid side against somebody's open pipe and quietly strand it. Nothing may run off the plate except through a vent of its own kind, and every fitting has to splice onto something already drawn. That last rule is why an empty plate has exactly twelve legal openings rather than three hundred: the vents are the only places anything can start, and from there you are steering one growing frontier instead of scattering pieces about. A vent pays nothing at all until its network reaches another vent, and networks pay far more per vent as they grow, so joining beats scattering — but the two things you want are at war with each other. Reaching a new vent means opening live ends, and every live end still facing an empty square when the run finishes is a penalty. The four running totals under the plate tick as you draw, and the loose-end figure reddens as the debt grows, so you watch it mount rather than have it sprung on you at the end. The real decision is not where a fitting goes, it is which of the four you spend first, because every mark rewrites what is legal for the rest of the round — and that is not a claim, it is the thing that was measured: an earlier version made you spend them in dealt order and a beam-searching bot scored identically to a one-ply greedy one on every seed in every mode, which is what a game with no decision in it looks like as a number. Three modes on three different axes. Trunk is the straight game: gather the edge into networks, where loops cost nothing and close two ends at once, and the coupler is the crown jewel because it is the only bridge between the six culvert vents and the six filament ones. Flume inverts the goal completely — vents pay nothing whatsoever, score is the length of each kind's longest unbroken run, and every stray cell off that run is charged — so every Trunk instinct is now wrong: a loop is pure waste, a tee cannot lengthen a path, and the coupler has become a trap that cuts a long run in two. Seep takes the lookahead away and hands you the fittings one at a time with no idea what is coming, and to make blind commitment survivable a mismatched join becomes legal at a price rather than refused, so you are never stuck holding something that fits nowhere. On a phone the grid square is never the commit target, which is the whole control scheme rather than a detail of it: a seven-by-seven plate at phone width gives about a fifty-pixel square and a fingertip covers roughly forty-five, so tapping a cell to place something would be a lottery. Instead, tapping a fitting tints every legal square at once and drops the piece on the best one, dragging anywhere on the plate only ever moves it — free and endlessly correctable — and the one control that actually writes anything is a sixty-pixel button down where your thumb already is. Rotate only ever cycles the orientations that are legal where the piece actually sits, and when the button is greyed it tells you why in plain words, because with no tutorial that sentence is where the rules are explained. Undo is unlimited within the round. Solo needs nobody: the page loads straight into the day's shared plate against two bots that drew the identical fittings, so there is a real table from the first mark and a par on screen from before you have played. Multiplayer is two to six over a shared link with no server and nobody in charge, because there is nothing to be in charge of — every device derives the fittings and the vents from one shared number and plays the run locally, so no game state ever crosses the network and nothing can desync. What crosses is a small ledger of which vents you have sealed and, at the end, your finished plate, purely so that everyone's summary can show everyone's plate. A player who goes quiet keeps the figures they last posted rather than freezing the table or vanishing from the comparison, and the summary lists the vents nobody at the table managed to reach. Huntress — A two-player territorial duel on a board that only ever gets smaller, and the whole thing rests on one move you can learn in a sentence. On your turn you slide one of your huntresses any distance in a straight line, exactly like a chess queen and through nothing, and then, from the square she has just landed on, she fires an arrow along another clear line. Wherever that arrow lands, the square burns, and it is gone for the rest of the game. Nobody is ever captured. You win when it is the other player's turn and there is nothing legal left for them to do, which means the game is not a chase — it is the slow business of taking the room away from somebody while making sure you keep enough of it for yourself. The move that turns it from a curiosity into a game is this: the square she came from is empty behind her, so it is a legal target for her own arrow. Run down a corridor and shoot the doorway shut, and the ground you just walked into belongs to you alone. That one trick is most of the game, and the board marks the doorway for you while you are choosing, because a player who has not spotted it has not found the game yet. Because every single turn burns exactly one square and nothing ever un-burns, the board is its own clock and the game cannot fail to end — the ceiling on its length is arithmetic rather than a hope. Late on, once neither side can reach the other at all, the rest would be bookkeeping, so the round is called there for whoever holds more ground rather than making you shuffle out your last twenty moves. Three grounds change the shape of a game rather than its difficulty. Thicket is six by six with two huntresses each, close enough that every arrow is felt on the very next turn. Moorland is seven by seven with three, with room to manoeuvre, so fencing off ground is the entire game. Greatwood is eight by eight with four — the long hunt, where you can lose a huntress into a corner and still win on territory. Weald — A map-drawing race for one to six built on a single fairness idea: nobody can be dealt a better hand than anybody else, because everybody is dealt exactly the same one. Every player gets the identical sequence of shapes, in the identical order, and draws each one anywhere on their own nine-by-nine map. You choose the spot, you choose which way round it goes — all eight turns and flips, free and reversible right up until you commit — and usually you choose which of four terrains it is made of. Then it is ink, and it is there for the rest of the run. What a finished map is worth is decided by four edicts, drawn from a pool of five and shuffled into a different order every game. Two are face up from the first turn and two are turned face down, revealing themselves part-way through, so the last quarter of a run is a change of plan rather than a lap of honour. Each edict scores twice, always over your whole map as it stands, which means a square you wrote in the first minute keeps paying and a square you regret keeps costing. Now and then the canker spreads, and this is the part that makes it a game rather than a puzzle: an identical patch of rot is dealt to everybody at the same moment, anchored to something on the board — it must touch water, or reach the rim, or come off a crag — and each of you carves it into your own map. It then bills you at the end of every watch for each unwritten square it is touching, so early rot is charged again and again, and the whole skill is where you choose to absorb damage that the entire table takes equally. Nothing ever fits nowhere, either: any card can be scrawled as a single square instead, and taking one clean square rather than five awkward ones is sometimes simply the better move. The three surveys swap the language a good map is written in rather than turning a difficulty dial. Canopy wants mass — big joined woods, one large lake, unwritten pockets you have walled in — so you extend what you have and cutting a lake in half is the mistake you are trying not to make. March is the exact inverse: it scores where two terrains touch, rewards Grove along the rim, and pays you for sealing the canker in, so blobbing is actively punished and the rot arrives in every single watch. Survey shrinks the map to eight by eight, which is the size at which finishing a whole row is actually reachable, and hands you two different shapes of the same size on every card instead of a big-or-small fork. That the three are genuinely different games is measured rather than claimed: a bot that optimises one survey's edicts while playing another loses between forty-eight and seventy-nine per cent of its score. Get the drag wrong and you drag again; there is no state in which a fat finger costs you a placement. The last one can be undone. Haggle — A two-player market duel built on one idea you can see in the first five seconds: the price of a ware is not a number somebody chose, it is a column of chits that you and the other trader both draw from, and it only ever goes down. Copper opens at eight, and printed underneath it, in plain sight, are the next three prices — six, five, four. Sell two Copper now and you bank fourteen. Wait for a third and you might bank nineteen, or you might come back to find the column two chits shorter because your rival sold first, and take twelve for the privilege of having been patient. That is the whole game, and it is the same question in a different shape every single turn. A plank table sits between you with five cards face up on it. Your turn is exactly one of four things: take a ware into your satchel, hire every porter standing on the table into your gang, barter two or more of your own cards for that many wares at once, or sell. Selling any number of one ware pays you the top chits off its column, highest first, and if a sale is at least three cards and pays at least ten it also takes the top bounty. Copper, Glass and Resin only sell two or more at a time, so the dear wares always cost you a commitment before they pay. Three empty columns ends the deal; most chits wins it, plus six for the bigger gang of porters and two for whoever did not move first; first to two pennants takes the match. The arithmetic is the design rather than decoration.
