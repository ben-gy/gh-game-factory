=== Web Games ===

Brood — A two-player abstract with no board at all, which is the first thing you notice and the thing everything else follows from. The tiles the two of you put down are the board, and it grows in whatever direction you push it. On your turn you do exactly one of two things: set a new tile down from your supply of eleven, or move one that is already on the table. A new tile has to touch one of your own and none of theirs, your Matriarch must be down by your fourth tile, and until she is, nothing of yours moves at all. You win by closing every side of the other Matriarch — and the sides can be closed by your tiles or by her own, which is the tension the whole game runs on, because building near your own Matriarch is a liability at the same time as it is a defence. Because the tiles are the board, they have to stay in one connected group, and that single constraint does most of the work: a tile holding the cluster together simply cannot move. It is drawn with stripes so you can see that at a glance rather than finding out by being refused, and the game will not even offer it to you. Every creature has its own law. The Strider walks the whole rim in one move and is the piece that actually closes rings. The Vaulter leaps over a line of tiles and lands in the first free space beyond, ignoring gaps too tight to walk through. The Climber steps up on top of a tile and pins whatever is underneath it, dead, for as long as it sits there. The Weaver takes exactly three steps around the outside and never doubles back. The Matriarch takes one. A walking tile also has to physically fit through the gap it slides across, so a space with tiles on both sides is closed to it — which matters more than it sounds, because the last open side of a nearly-surrounded Matriarch is usually exactly that kind of space. Three rule sets, all fielding the same eleven tiles so none of them is secretly the big one. Clutch is the plain game. Retinue trades a Strider for a Tender, who instead of moving can lift a touching tile of either colour over herself into a free space beside her — and because she carries it over the top rather than sliding it, she is the only piece in the game that can plug that unreachable last space, or pull it open again once you have. Cinch changes one word: five closed sides is enough. That does not merely make it shorter, it inverts how you think, because a tile of your own parked beside your own Matriarch is now two fifths of a loss and you find yourself developing away from her instead of around her. Solo you play a real search engine, not a placeholder, at three strengths — Hatchling will genuinely miss things, Forager reads the ring properly, and Broodmother closes nets three moves before you see them — and it answers against a clock so it stays responsive on a phone. Whether the game is still a game was measured rather than argued: a few hundred bot-vs-bot games from randomised openings, played both ways round so no seat can hide an advantage, with the numbers reported alongside their confidence intervals. Two things the designers were confident about did not survive contact with those numbers and were dropped. Multiplayer is peer-to-peer for two with no server and no authoritative host at all, which works because there is nothing to be authoritative about — nothing random, nothing hidden. Every browser sends its whole move list on every move, about a quarter of a kilobyte for an entire game, so a lost message is a non-event, someone arriving late is instantly caught up, and a browser running a different version is detected and told rather than quietly drifting out of step. Join by scanning a QR, opening a link or typing the four-character code. If the other player closes their tab you do not sit on a frozen board — you get a visible countdown, the reason, and then the win. No accounts, no cookies, no tracking beyond an anonymous page count. [board, abstract, versus, multiplayer, mobile] https://brood.benrichardson.dev

Bracken — A racing-and-blocking abstract for two to four, and the whole game is one either-or you face every single turn. You start in the middle of your own edge of a square common and you are trying to reach the edge opposite. On your turn you do exactly one of two things: walk one square, or plant a hedge to make somebody else's route longer. That is it, and the tension falls straight out of it — every hedge you plant is a step you did not take, so slowing the leader always costs you tempo, and a hedge is permanent, so the wall you build to solve this turn is still standing when you need to come back through it. The rule that keeps it a race rather than a walling contest is that you may lengthen a route as far as you like but never seal one off entirely: there is always a way through, and the only question is how expensive you can make it. You never even see an illegal hedge, because of how the board is aimed. A hedge does not sit in a square — it lies on the lattice between them, spanning two squares' worth of the way through, which on a phone is about five pixels of target. So nothing here is hit-tested by its own outline: every tap resolves to the nearest thing you are actually allowed to do, which means there is no dead zone anywhere on the board, the hedge that would trap somebody is not merely refused but unaimable, and the rare crowded case where two legal steps sit a single square apart asks you to confirm rather than guessing. Tap roughly where you want the hedge, turn it if you meant the other way, plant it. Three commons change how it plays rather than how big it is. Heath is nine by nine with ten hedges each — long routes, room to build a real maze, and an early hedge still shaping the race twenty moves later. Fold is seven by seven with six, so every hedge is a bigger fraction of a shorter route and it is sharp, tactical and over in half the moves. Coppice is nine by nine but replaces both supplies with one shared stack of twelve, so you are not spending your hedges, you are spending the hedges: a mediocre block can be exactly right purely to take it out of a rival's hands, and once the pile runs dry the maze is fixed and the rest is a pure footrace. Solo you play a real search opponent at three strengths — Rambler blunders, Poacher is a genuine game, and Gamekeeper reads several moves ahead and will punish a lazy walk — and it thinks against a clock rather than a fixed depth, so it stays responsive on a phone instead of freezing it. Whether the player who moves first has an edge was measured rather than assumed, and the measurement changed the design: a first pass over sixty games said the small board was badly unfair and that an extra hedge for the second player fixed it, and six hundred games across two independent seed families said the even game was already fair and that the fix would have handed the advantage to the other player, so there is no compensation rule at all. Multiplayer is peer-to-peer for two to four with no server and no authoritative host, which it can manage because there is nothing to be authoritative about — no dice, no shuffling, nothing hidden. Every browser sends its whole move list on every move, so a lost message is a non-event, somebody arriving late is instantly caught up, and a player who leaves is written into that same list rather than guessed at. Join by scanning a QR, opening a link or typing the four-letter code. No accounts, no cookies, no tracking beyond an anonymous page count. Shunt — A train-yard puzzle, and the whole thing rests on one physical fact you can see in the first five seconds. A locomotive stands light on the headshunt, a few dead-end roads hold wagons, and an order slip names four or five of them and the exact order they must stand in on the departure road. There are only two things you can do. Tap a road and the loco propels whatever it is coupled to straight in. Tap a wagon and it draws that wagon, and everything between it and the loco, back out. That is the entire control scheme, and each of those trips is one move — because each is a change of direction, which is the expensive thing in a real yard, so the game is about planning trips rather than shifting as many wagons as you can. The consequence is the puzzle: because the loco always couples at the near end, the order of a rake never reverses. A wagon you want in front of one you are already holding cannot simply be swapped; it has to go the long way round, parked in a third road while you fetch the other, and then collected again. Everything else is a variation on that one constraint. Three yards change the shape of the problem rather than its size. Sidings is three stub roads and six wagons, the plain form of the manoeuvre. Run-round adds a loop that lets the loco reach the far end of its rake and turn it back-to-front — the one thing a flat yard cannot do at any price — for two moves, so the question becomes whether turning a rake is worth what it costs; measured over thirty yards, taking the loop away makes almost half of them strictly harder. Kickback hides one road behind another, reachable only by standing in Road A first, so half the yard is behind a door and every wagon you leave in the way is a wagon in your own way. The number that matters is par, and it is not an estimate. Yards are generated by searching outward from every finished train, which makes each one guaranteed solvable and its par the proven shortest solve that exists — audited by a second, independent search that shares nothing with the first, plus a control proving no shorter solve is possible. So "on par" really is as good as anyone could have done, and when you finish you can watch the best line played back move by move. Setting back a move is free and unlimited, as often as you like, because a mis-tap on a phone should never cost you a yard; finish without using it for a clean solve. Today's yard is the same for everyone on Earth and changes at midnight UTC, or take a random one, or share a seed so a friend shunts the identical yard. Multiplayer is a race on identical ground for two to six, and there is no server and no authoritative host at all: every browser builds the same yard from the shared seed and shunts its own copy, so no yard state is ever transmitted — only a few numbers. Join by scanning a QR, opening a link or typing the four-letter code, and the results screen shows every player against the same proven par, including anyone who did not finish. Close the host's tab mid-round and everyone else carries straight on. No accounts, no cookies, no tracking beyond an anonymous page count. Clatter — A dice roll-and-write built on one rule that will not let you be selfish quietly. On your turn a pool of six dice is rolled and you take exactly one — and every die in the pool smaller than the one you took spills onto the table, where every other player takes one and writes it on their own sheet. Grab the six you need to close your Run and you have just handed three rivals their pick of five dice; take the one your Ladder has been waiting for and nothing spills at all, but a player who gets nothing banks a shim, a wildcard that nudges a later die up or down by one. Greed feeds them dice, denial feeds them flexibility, and there is no third option — which is the whole game, and it is legible the first time you watch the tray mark the dice you are about to give away while you are still holding the die. Your sheet has four interlocking tracks: a Ladder that only ever accepts a higher face, so opening it on a one buys you the whole climb; a Tally of six columns two deep where every face has a home until it hasn't; a Run with rising floors where the big dice go; and a set of Weights where a pair opened with a two prints the five it wants and will accept nothing else. The marked cells grant free marks in the other tracks, so one well-chosen die can cascade three deep, and because every free mark lands on a fixed square the cascade never stops to ask you a question. Three modes play differently rather than bigger: Workshop (six dice, twenty-four turns, room to spread across all four tracks), Scramble (five dice, half the turns, and only your best two tracks count, so there is no time to spread and you must commit), and Tribute (every rival who takes a die out of your spill pays you a point, which inverts the central question from how little you can give away to how much it is worth selling). Every player gets exactly the same number of dice whatever the player count, so the game is the same shape with two of you as with four, and the earlier seats start with a couple of points to cancel a turn-order advantage that was measured rather than guessed. Solo you play the identical game against bots at three strengths that price what your spill hands each rival off their sheet rather than yours. Multiplayer is peer-to-peer for two to four with no server and no host at all: every die comes from a shared seed so no roll is ever sent, every message carries the whole game, and because no peer is authoritative the host leaving is a non-event and a dropped message is unobservable. Join by scanning a QR, opening a link or typing the four-letter code. Tap a die, then tap a track. No accounts, no cookies, no tracking beyond an anonymous page count. [card, dice, roll-and-write, versus, multiplayer] https://clatter.benrichardson.dev

Tacit — A co-operative trick-taking card game for one to five, and the whole thing lives in the gap between what you can see and what you are allowed to say. Everyone is dealt an equal hand, a few task cards are laid face up, and you take turns claiming one each — but never a card that is already in your own hand, so every single task in the game depends on somebody else putting it where you can reach it. Then you play tricks the ordinary way: follow the colour that was led if you can, highest card of that colour takes it, unless somebody plays a Gantry, which trumps everything. A task is complete only when the crewmate who claimed it wins the trick containing that exact card. If anyone else takes it, the mission is over for everybody at once. So you sit there watching a crewmate lead the colour you are holding their card in, deciding in silence whether they are asking you for it or about to lose it — and you cannot ask. In Aside you get one signal for the entire mission: show a single card as your highest, lowest or only one of its colour, and the game will not let you lie, because it only ever offers you the claims that are actually true of your hand. In Hush you get nothing at all and read the whole table off the cards that hit it. In Running Order every task is numbered and must land in sequence, which inverts the game — most of the round is spent dodging cards you badly want, and a card that cannot legally land yet is locked and cannot be played, unless following suit leaves you nothing else and the game warns you first. Clear a mission and the crew climbs a rung: more tasks, then numbered chains, then one that must land in the very final trick. Fail and the run ends. Solo you play with a crew of bots that genuinely cannot see your hand — that is enforced by the code and tested, not promised — and they have no difficulty tiers on purpose, because in a co-operative game a weaker teammate is a harder game and every loss would read as your crew being stupid. Multiplayer is peer-to-peer for two to five with no server at all: create a room and a friend can scan the QR, open the link or type the four-letter code. Hands stay genuinely hidden, because the host shuffles from a private seed it never transmits and sends each player only their own cards, while everything public travels as one shared log. If the host leaves mid-mission the crew re-attests their hands to whoever takes over and the run carries on; if only one player is left, that is a walkover rather than an endless wait. No accounts, no cookies, no tracking beyond an anonymous page count. [card, co-op, trick-taking, deduction, multiplayer] https://tacit.benrichardson.dev

Tithe — A two-player duel on a small stone court where the only moves that exist are the two cards in your hand. Each card — a toll — prints a little pattern of jumps, and to move any piece at all you must spend one of your two. That is where the whole game lives, because the toll you spend does not go away: it goes onto the table between you, and you pick up whichever one was already sitting there. So the tool you just used is the tool your opponent gets to use next. Every turn is two questions at once — which square do I want, and which of my two tools can I afford to hand over — and a move that wins a piece while arming the enemy with the exact jump they need is not a good move. You have five pieces, a Keeper wearing a gold ring and four Bearers, and you win either by taking their Keeper or by walking your own onto their coffer, the marked square in the middle of their back row; both are live from the first turn. Two more rules earn their keep. The game opens with a pie cut: one player divides the dealt tolls into two pairs and the other picks a pair — and moves first, so cutting them unevenly hands your opponent the better hand and the tempo together. And the levy: go too long with nothing being taken and the tide collects, removing the rearmost Bearer from whoever has more pieces standing, so sitting on a lead is the one thing the game punishes. Three modes change the rules rather than the size: Ledger (the steady deck with everyone on the back row, a jam where the real currency is what you can bear to give away), Spree (the wild deck of long leaps with two Bearers already pushed up, contact on turn one), and Arrears (two tolls on the table, and you may never reclaim the one you just paid, so every turn you also choose what to leave lying around). Solo you play a real engine — alpha-beta search with iterative deepening over the same rules you are playing, at three strengths, and it never blunders a win at any of them. Multiplayer is a peer-to-peer duel for two: join by scanning a QR, opening a link or typing the room code, and both browsers run identical rules from a shared seed with only the move list crossing the wire. Genuinely serverless — no game server, no account — and if your opponent's browser vanishes the round is held for a moment and then finishes as a walkover, so you always reach the results, which show both players' breakdowns and any win either of you left on the board. Tap a piece, tap a square. No accounts, no cookies, no tracking beyond an anonymous page count. [board, abstract, versus, hand-management] https://tithe.benrichardson.dev

Skyhold — A two-player game of building and climbing on a small grid, and a turn is only two halves: move one of your builders one square in any direction, then raise one block on any empty square beside it. Towers grow from the ground to one, two, three storeys, and a fourth block caps a tower with a dome nobody can ever enter again. You may climb at most one storey at a time but you may drop as far as you like, and the instant one of your builders steps from a second storey onto a third, you win the sky — you also win by leaving your rival with no legal turn at all. That is the whole tension: every block you place is a staircase for you and a wall for them at the same moment, so the game is won by building a fork, two live climbs your opponent can only cap one of per turn. Rounds open on a board that already has stonework on it, laid symmetrically so neither side is handed the better half, and you each place your builders one at a time — whoever places first moves second, which is the measured split that keeps the opening fair. Three modes play genuinely differently: Keep (5×5, two builders, the pure tempo duel where every threat has an answer), Charter (each side drafts a power tile with a budget of charges — build twice, cap a dome at any height, shove a rival back, move twice, or build beside your other builder — so blocking is no longer sufficient and the skill becomes baiting a spend), and Highlands (6×6 with three builders each, too much board to cover, where forks open and towers get abandoned). Solo, you play a real opponent: a searching engine with three tiers, from one that climbs and blocks but will never set a trap, to one that closes the trap three turns before you see it — and the results screen shows the turn where the game was actually decided, or says honestly that there wasn't one. Multiplayer is a peer-to-peer duel for two: join by scanning a QR, opening a link or typing the room code, and both browsers run the identical rules with only the move list crossing the wire. It's genuinely serverless — no game server, no account — and if your opponent's browser vanishes the round ends as a walkover so you always reach the results, never a frozen board; "play again" starts a fresh round in the same room with the seats swapped and the match tally carrying across. Tap a builder, tap where it goes, tap where to build; undo stays live until you commit. No accounts, no cookies, no tracking beyond an anonymous page count. Cairn — A chain-clearing solitaire, dressed as dismantling a cairn: a pyramid of runed stones, each a card rank from ace to king. One stone sits in your hand, and you take any uncovered stone whose rune is one step above or below it — aces wrap around to kings — so it becomes your new hand and frees the stones it was sitting on. Take stones one after another without stopping and a heat multiplier climbs, so a long unbroken chain is worth far more than the same stones picked off one at a time; when nothing is takeable you draw from the scree pile, which hands you a fresh stone but resets your heat and spends one of a limited number of draws. Every cairn is generated with a full-clear solution built in, so you are never dealt an impossible board — the skill is reading the runes and hoarding your draws. Three modes change the rule that ends a run, not just the size: Ramble (one cairn, no clock, a generous scree — clear the mountain at your pace and chase a high score on chains), Cascade (a stream of small cairns against a sixty-second clock, banking as many as you can), and Scree (one big cairn on a hard draw budget — run out of draws with stones still standing and you are buried). It's genuinely serverless: every device builds the same cairn from a shared seed, only a tiny status ever crosses the wire, and if the host leaves the round still finishes for everyone. Tap a stone to take it and tap the scree to draw, or use the arrow keys with Enter and D. No accounts, no cookies, no tracking beyond an anonymous page count. Skein — An untangle puzzle. A web of glowing beads is strung together by threads that cross in a tangled mess; you grab a bead, drag it somewhere better, and the crossings it was part of wink out. Every web is built so a tangle-free solution always exists, so you are never stuck — and fewer moves is a better solve. Hatch — A picross puzzle, where numbers hide a picture. Down the side of each row and above each column is a little list of numbers, and they simply count the filled squares: "3 1" means a run of three, a gap, then a lone one. Work out from those clues which squares must be filled, paint them in, and a hidden picture emerges square by square. The catch that makes it a game of nerve as much as logic: marking a square as empty is a free, reversible note, but actually filling one is a commitment — fill a square that's really empty and it's a mistake, and it costs you — so it rewards reading the numbers over guessing. Every board is built to be solvable by pure deduction, never a lucky guess, with a single unique answer. Brim — A hold-your-nerve party game built around the one sensor these games had never used: the phone's accelerometer, reading how still you hold it.
